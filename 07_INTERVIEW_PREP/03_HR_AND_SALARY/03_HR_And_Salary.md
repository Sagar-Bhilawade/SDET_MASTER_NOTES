# HR & Salary — Negotiation, Notice, and Why You're Leaving

## What it is

The HR round: CTC negotiation, notice period, why you're leaving, background-verification (BGV) reality, and the career-pivot story if it comes up. This round rarely decides *whether* you get an offer — but it decides *how much the offer is for*, and a fumble here can cost you 2-4 LPA on the number that matters most.

---

## Why it matters

You're at 13.1 LPA targeting 20-24. That's a 55-85% jump, which is achievable but only if you negotiate deliberately. The HR conversation is where most engineers give the money back — by anchoring low, apologizing for their range, or revealing their current CTC before they have leverage. Your technical rounds earn the offer; this round sizes it.

**The BGV reality that shapes everything:** at the 20-24 band, background verification *will* check your dates and likely your last-drawn salary. Your resume dates are trivially verifiable. So the strategy is never to lie about numbers — it's to negotiate on *scope and market rate*, and to let your genuine strengths (rare Playwright depth, two-stack range, certifications) justify the jump.

---

## The salary conversation

### The core answer

> "Based on the scope of this role and my experience across both a modern Playwright stack and a legacy Selenium one, I'm looking in the range of 20 to 24. I'm open to discussing where in that range makes sense given the full package."

Calm, no apology, anchored on scope. Then **stop talking.** The silence after a number is theirs to fill, not yours.

### The rules

| Rule | Why |
|------|-----|
| **Let them ask first if you can** | Whoever names a number first anchors — but if pushed, you name your *target*, not your current |
| **Anchor on the range, not a point** | "20 to 24" leaves room; "22" gets negotiated down to 20 |
| **Never apologize or over-explain** | "I know it's a big jump, but..." hands back your leverage |
| **Talk total CTC, then components** | Base vs variable vs joining bonus matters; a 24 with 40% variable isn't a 24 |
| **Current CTC is 13.1 — state it plainly if asked** | It's verifiable at BGV. Don't inflate it. Pivot immediately to market rate |

### When they ask "what's your current CTC?"

Answer honestly, then reframe fast:

> "My current fixed is 13.1. I'll be candid that it's below market for 4+ years of SDET work across manual and automation testing with framework ownership across two stacks — which is part of why I'm looking. What I'm focused on is the value for this role, and that's the 20-24 range."

Honesty on the number + immediate pivot to market rate. You're not hiding 13.1 — you're framing it as *the reason you're worth more elsewhere*, which is true.

### When they push back: "that's a big hike over your current"

> "It is relative to my current, and I'd argue my current is the anomaly, not my ask. If you look at what the market pays for real Playwright framework experience plus a legacy stack and ISTQB/Azure certification, 20-24 is where it sits. I'm not asking for a premium — I'm asking to be paid market for the role you're hiring for."

Never concede that 13.1 is your "real" worth. It's the below-market baseline you're leaving.

## Why you're leaving

**The iron rule: never criticize NielsenIQ.** Complaining about your current employer signals you'll do the same about them, and it makes you sound like the problem left, not the opportunity.

Forward-looking, always:

> "I've had genuinely good range at NielsenIQ — I built in the legacy Selenium stack and then owned framework design on the modern Playwright side. What I'm looking for now is more scope on the architecture and strategy side: owning the quality approach across teams, mentoring, being the person making tooling decisions rather than implementing someone else's. That's the direction I've been moving, and I want to do it with more ownership than my current scope allows."

No complaints. It also quietly justifies the senior title and the salary jump — you're growing into a bigger role, not fleeing a bad one.

## Notice period

Be straight and specific. Know your actual notice (typically 60-90 days in Indian IT).

> "My notice is [X] days. I'm happy to explore whether it can be brought down through buyout or accrued leave, and I'll be transparent about the timeline once I have an offer to work with."

If they need someone faster and you have flexibility (buyout), that's a negotiation chip — use it, don't give it away free.

## The career-pivot story (if it comes up)

Your background — civil engineering B.Tech, then PG-DAC at Sunbeam, then SDET — is a *strength* when framed as deliberate, not accidental. This is legitimate HR-round material when kept professional, focused, and resume-backed.

> "My degree was in civil engineering, but I moved into software deliberately — did the PG-DAC program at Sunbeam Pune to build the CS foundation properly, and came into testing early. I found the engineering side of QA — framework design, CI, systems thinking — was genuinely where I was strongest, and I've built my whole career there since. The pivot wasn't drift; it was a decision I committed to and it paid off."

Owns the pivot as intentional. Shows self-awareness and follow-through — both senior signals.

Keep the answer professional and NielsenIQ-focused. Do not add unrelated private-work details or personal financial context; they distract from the SDET story and create negotiation noise for zero upside.

## Common interview Q&A

**Q: "What are your salary expectations?"**
The core answer: 20-24, anchored on scope, no apology, then stop talking.

**Q: "Do you have other offers?"**
If yes and real: mention it calmly as context, don't weaponize it. *"I'm in a couple of processes, but this role is a strong fit for what I'm looking for."* If no: *"I'm actively interviewing and being selective about fit."* Never invent a fake offer — it can collapse spectacularly.

**Q: "Why should we pay you 20+ when your current is 13?"**
The market-rate reframe above. Your current is the anomaly.

**Q: "Where do you see yourself in five years?"**
*"Deeper on architecture and strategy — owning the quality approach across multiple teams rather than one suite."* Direction without "I'll leave in a year."

**Q: "What's your biggest weakness?"**
A real one with a fix: *"I've defaulted to solving problems myself rather than delegating — faster short-term, but it doesn't build the team, so I've been deliberately handing over ownership even when it's slower at first."*

**Q: "Are you open to relocation / hybrid / WFO?"**
Know your answer in advance (Pune-based). Be honest — a mismatch found later wastes everyone's time and can void an offer.

## Weak areas to watch

- **Don't reveal your target before you have leverage.** If you can get them to state a band first, do.
- **Don't inflate current CTC.** 13.1 is verifiable at BGV. Inflating it is the one move that can void an offer after you've resigned — catastrophic. Frame it as below-market instead.
- **Don't apologize for your range.** The moment you say "I know it's a lot" you've lost 2 LPA.
- **Don't badmouth NielsenIQ.** Forward-looking only.
- **Don't add unrelated private-work context.** There is no version of that disclosure that helps you in an SDET interview.
- **Don't accept on the call.** *"Thank you — I'd like to review the full written offer before confirming."* Always.

---

## Key takeaway

> **Negotiate on scope and market rate, never on your current CTC — because your current is the anomaly you're leaving, and it's the one number BGV can check.** State 20-24 calmly, anchor on the value of the role, and stop talking. Why-you're-leaving stays forward-looking, and the pivot story is a strength when owned as deliberate.
